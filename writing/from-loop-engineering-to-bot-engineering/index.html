From Loop Engineering to Bot Engineering

Chengzhuo Tong November 26, 2026

Long-Horizon Execution, Context Rot, and the Two Kinds of Bots

I want to lay out a core idea I have been thinking about recently. Over the past while, much of our discussion has moved from the earliest Ralph loop to loop engineering, graph engineering, and various AutoResearch frameworks. At heart, all of these efforts around loops share a single goal: to sustain genuine long-horizon execution and push a task forward over time. The central obstacle to long-horizon execution, however, is context. Because of this, the standard approach has been to rely on a while true loop that keeps state external and periodically spawns a new session inside the loop. That pattern keeps the work moving forward while preventing context rot and context drift.

With the recent rise of the Bot as a distinct paradigm, however, I have started looking at loops differently. To begin with, I think Bots fall into two categories. The first is the familiar personal agent Bot, which draws on your full personal context to handle the various trigger signals of daily life. Yet the much larger opportunity lies in the second kind of Bot—for instance, in Grok Bot, where you can spin up multiple new Bots and assign each one to anchor and drive a specific initiative. Tasks of this nature are defined primarily by their temporal dimension.

Many of the tasks we hand to models today are spatial tasks. By a spatial task, I mean a one-off job that can be completed in a single pass. A temporal task, by contrast, has two defining characteristics: either it recurs repeatedly, or it requires an extended period of time—days, weeks, or even months—to see through to completion. In short, any task that demands a long timescale and persists over an extended duration is a temporal task. Working Bots are built specifically to solve this problem.

Bots as Pre-Built Loops and Graphs with Long-Term Memory

Once we recognize this distinction, it becomes clear why so much effort is now pouring into Bot frameworks—such as Orca, OpenBot, and others—that support working Bots. Because these frameworks exist, we no longer need to indulge the urge to hand-roll custom loop workflows from scratch, such as a classic Ralph loop built around a while loop, external state files, and periodic new sessions. Instead of reinventing the wheel, we are much better off reusing the Bot frameworks others have already engineered. The crucial point is that even if these frameworks are still rough around the edges today, they will steadily improve—and they will ultimately become the most effective vehicle for driving long-horizon tasks.

At a structural level, a pre-built Bot today is equivalent to what we used to construct as a loop: one Bot equals one loop. By extension, a collection of Bots forms a graph, and the interactions among Bots correspond to the edges and relationships of graph engineering. Compared with building custom loops over and over again, the single greatest advantage of adopting Bots directly is that the underlying framework already provides a persistent long-term memory mechanism, sparing you from having to rebuild that infrastructure yourself.

Orchestrating Sub Agents and Subbots

This brings me to a related observation about orchestration frameworks. Today's agent orchestration frameworks typically dispatch Sub Agents to execute spatial tasks. In the future, however, orchestration frameworks should also be able to dispatch Subbots—assigning them to tasks that span both space and time. Some tasks appear on the surface to have no temporal dimension, yet if you look past the immediate request to the underlying need—and reframe that need into something more ambitious and imaginative—their temporal nature becomes obvious. For example, you might dispatch a Sub Agent to perform Deep Research once on a given topic. But your deeper, more fundamental need may be to have a dedicated domain expert in that field: an entity that conducts deep research on a regular schedule, routinely reviews materials across different sources, gradually learns and matures much like a human researcher, develops genuine insight, and reports back to you periodically.

That is the trajectory I expect orchestration frameworks to follow: evolving from orchestrating only Sub Agents to orchestrating and dispatching both Sub Agents and Subbots side by side. The distinction between an Agent and a Bot comes down to two things. First is the nature of the task: whether it is confined to a single spatial execution or spans both time and space. Second is that a Bot possesses its own memory and capacity for reflection, making it an entity capable of continuous growth. A single session simply does not have those qualities—and yet those are the very qualities required to take on far more ambitious work.

This shift also changes how we approach macro-level objectives. When tackling a large, overarching task, we can draw on the concept of Anthropic's (Claude's) dynamic workflows—except that instead of orchestrating only one-off Sub Agents as in the past, both dynamic workflows and static workflows will now be able to orchestrate Sub Agents and Subbots side by side. Given a grand objective, you can start by consulting a frontier model to work out how one-off Sub Agents and a team of resident Bots should divide the labor.

How to Prompt a Bot: Prompting with a Vision vs. Acceptance Criteria

Finally, at the level of each individual Bot, this raises a practical question: how does prompting a Bot differ from prompting an Agent? First, we need to be clear about one thing: a Bot is not a router. A Bot is not merely a tool for routing your messages across different sessions; if you only use it as a router, you would be much better off just managing your work in Linear—using a Bot as a router is the lowest-leverage way to use it. Likewise, prompting a Bot the same way you would prompt an ordinary Agent simply does not work.

To my mind, the most effective way to prompt a Bot is to give it a task that spans a long timescale and is deeply ambitious and challenging—in other words, a somewhat more open-ended, more ambitious objective, the kind of task we often call a Vision. When you prompt an Agent, by contrast, you need to provide clear, explicit, and concrete acceptance criteria.

从 Loop 到 Bot：空间型任务与时间型任务

我想先聊聊最近的一个核心想法。过去一段时间，大家讨论了很多概念，从最早的 Ralph loop，到 loop engineering、graph engineering，再到很多 AutoResearch 框架。本质上，它们在 loop 这件事上做文章，都是为了能够真正以 long-horizon（长周期）的方式去持续推进一件事情。但要做 long-horizon 的持续推进，最大的问题就是 context（上下文）。因此，绝大多数的做法依然是使用一个 while true 循环，把状态保存在外部，然后在循环里定期开启新的 session，以此来保证任务能够一直往前推进，同时防止 context rot（上下文腐烂）和上下文漂移。

不过，结合最近 Bot 这个概念的兴起，我对 loop 这件事有了新的看法。我个人认为，Bot 首先可以分为两种。第一种是大家常见意义上的 personal agent Bot，它调用你的全部个人 context，主要帮你处理生活中的各种触发信号。但 Bot 更大的用途在于第二种——比如在 Grok Bot 里面，你可以设置很多个新的 Bot，让每一个新 Bot 专门去锚定和主推一件事情。这类事情通常在时间维度上具有很强的延续性。

如果从任务属性来看，很多传统任务其实是“空间型任务”。所谓空间型任务，就是单次性的任务，让它跑一次就能做完。那什么是“时间型任务”呢？时间型任务有两种情况：要么是它会反复发生；要么是它需要很长的一段时间——比如几天、几个星期，甚至几个月——才能真正做完。总而言之，凡是在时间维度上需要漫长周期、在很长一段时间里都会持续存在的任务，就是时间型任务。而那种适合工作场景的 Bot，主要就是用来解决时间型任务的。

不要重复造轮子：一个 Bot 即 Loop，一个群组即 Graph

想清楚这一点之后就会发现，现在大家基本上都在卷各种支持工作型 Bot 的框架，包括 Orca、OpenBot 以及其他一些 Bot 框架。这意味着，往后大家其实没必要再手痒去从头手搓很多那样的 workflow 了——最典型的就是像 Ralph loop 那样，自己写一个 while 循环，把状态文件往外存，再在里面定期开新 session。最好的做法是不要反复造轮子，直接去复用别人已经搭好的优质 Bot 框架。因为最重要的是，我相信这些框架即便今天还不够完善，未来也一定会越来越好，在长期推进一项任务这件事上，它们最终一定能做到最好。

所以这里的核心逻辑是：别人今天已经搭好的一个 Bot，其实就等于我们过去手写的一个 loop——一个 Bot 就等于一个 loop；而一堆 Bot 组合在一起，就等于一个 graph；Bot 和 Bot 之间的联系，其实就是 graph 里面的连接关系，也就是过去 graph engineering 里处理的关系。直接复用 Bot 而不是自己反复造轮子去写 loop，最大的好处就在于别人已经帮你搭好了那一套长效的 memory（长期记忆）机制，你完全不需要再自己重复搭建了。

从分发 Sub Agent 到同时编排 Subbot

再补充一个关于编排框架的观点。现在的 Agent 编排框架，分发出去的 Sub Agent 往往只是去执行一些空间型任务。但我认为，未来的编排框架应该同时能够分发 Subbot，让 Subbot 去承担那些时间跨度和空间属性都很强的任务。有些任务表面上看起来似乎没有什么时间属性，但如果你回归到需求背后的本质需求，并且把这个需求变成一件更有野心、更有想法的事情，情况就完全不同了。举个例子，你可以派一个 Sub Agent 去帮你对某个课题做一次 deep research；但也许你真正的根本需求，是希望拥有一个该领域的专业专家。它应该每隔一段时间就定期去做 deep research，定期去翻看各个地方的资料，像人一样逐渐学习、逐渐成长，最终变成一个很有 insight（洞见）的角色，并定期向你汇报。

这正是我所说的演进方向：未来的 Agent 框架会从单纯编排 Sub Agent，走向同时编排和分发 Sub Agent 与 Subbot。总结来看，Agent 和 Bot 的区别主要体现在两方面：第一是任务属性的区别，即它完成的是单纯空间维度上的任务，还是在时间和空间维度上都存在跨度的任务；第二是 Bot 具备自己的 memory 和反思机制，是一个能够不断成长的实体。这种特质是单条 session 所不具备的，而恰恰是这种特质，能够帮助它完成更多、更具野心的事情。

沿着这个思路，当你面对一个非常宏观的任务时，完全可以借鉴 Anthropic（Claude）动态工作流的思想；只不过以前只能编排单纯一次性运行的 Sub Agent，而未来无论是动态工作流还是静态工作流，都可以同时编排 Sub Agent 和 Subbot。具体来说，当你有一个宏大的目标时，可以先和一个能力很强的模型讨论，商量这些一次性运行的 Sub Agent 与常驻的 Bot 应该如何分工。

How to Prompt a Bot：用 Vision 提示 Bot，用验收标准提示 Agent

最后，到了具体每一个 Bot 的层面，就会涉及一个核心问题：How to prompt a Bot，和 How to prompt an Agent 有什么不同？首先要明确一点：A Bot is not a router（Bot 不是路由器）。Bot 绝不是一个单纯帮你把消息路由到不同会话的工具；如果只把它当成 Router 来用，那完全不如直接用 Linear 管理更方便——把 Bot 当 Router 是最 low 的用法。同样，把 Bot 当成普通 Agent 一样去提示，也是非常不行的做法。

我认为最大的区别在于，真正提示 Bot 最有效的方法，是给它一个在时间尺度上比较久、非常有野心（ambitious）和挑战性的任务——也就是给它一些相对模糊一点、但更具野心的目标，这样的任务我们往往称之为 Vision（愿景）；而对于一个 Agent，你则需要给它清晰、明确、具体的验收方式。
